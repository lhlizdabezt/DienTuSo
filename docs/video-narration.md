### 00:20-00:48 - Architecture and operating modes

> The 555 timer generates the clock. A mode selector then chooses automatic or manual operation. In automatic mode, the clock drives the synchronous counter continuously. In manual mode, an SR latch stores the traffic operator's request, and the gated clock advances the same counter only after authorization. Both modes therefore preserve one deterministic lamp sequence.

### 00:48-01:22 - Counter design method

> The controller follows the optimized state cycle eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve, zero, one, two, three, and four. This realizes two four-count green intervals and two one-count yellow intervals. We derived the present-state and next-state table, applied the JK excitation table, minimized every input with Karnaugh maps, and implemented the resulting equations directly in the circuit.

### 01:22-01:52 - Output decoding and safety

> The lamp decoder depends only on Q three and Q two. Entries A and C share one phase, while B and D share the opposite phase. Their green equations are mutually exclusive because one contains Q three and the other contains its complement. Therefore, the two approach pairs can never receive green simultaneously, including during unused counter states.

### 01:52-02:18 - Falstad verification

> In Falstad, the automatic sequence repeats A-C green, A-C yellow, B-D green, and B-D yellow. Manual mode holds the displayed phase until the operator enables the next advance. The oscilloscope confirms the timer waveform and clock behavior, and all unused states recover to the valid sequence after one selected edge.

### 02:18-02:35 - Hardware and conclusion

> The breadboard prototype reproduces the same four-one-four-one sequence on four three-color signal heads. Automatic cycling, manual hold, authorized advance, and conflict-free green outputs were verified on the bench. This completes the path from Boolean design and simulation to physical implementation. Thank you for watching.

### 01:05-01:55 - Functional architecture

**Speaker:** Lương Hải Long

**On screen:** Show Figure 2 in the report. Trace the arrows slowly from left to right.

**Narration:**

> The architecture follows the required clock flow. First, the pulse generator creates the oscillator clock. The mode selector then separates two paths. When M equals zero, the automatic path transfers the oscillator clock directly to the synchronous counter. When M equals one, the manual path waits for permission from the traffic operator. The manual-control latch stores that permission, and the gate passes an authorized clock pulse to the same counter. Both paths therefore use one state machine and one lamp decoder. Changing the operating mode changes the source of counter edges, but it does not change the lamp sequence.

### 01:55-02:45 - Pulse-generator block

**Speaker:** Lương Hải Long

**On screen:** Zoom to the 555 timer, the 10-kilohm feedback resistor, the 300-nanofarad capacitor, and the left oscilloscope trace.

**Narration:**

> The pulse generator is a 555 relaxation oscillator. The timing capacitor repeatedly charges and discharges between the internal threshold levels. For this symmetric feedback arrangement, the first-order period is two times the natural logarithm of two, multiplied by the feedback resistance and the capacitance. Therefore, the frequency is the reciprocal of that period. With a 10-kilohm feedback resistor and a 300-nanofarad capacitor, the calculated frequency is approximately 240.45 hertz. Falstad measures about 239.017 hertz, so the difference is approximately 0.60 percent. This clock is intentionally fast for demonstration; a real traffic installation would require a slower, safety-qualified timing source.

### 02:45-03:35 - Automatic/manual selector and manual logic

**Speaker:** Lương Hải Long

**On screen:** Zoom to the multiplexer, the two cross-coupled NOR gates, the request control, and the AND gate.

**Narration:**

> The two-input multiplexer selects either the continuous oscillator clock or the manually gated clock. The manual-control block contains two cross-coupled NOR gates, which form an active-high SR latch. A momentary request sets the latch and stores the operator's permission. The AND gate combines this stored enable with the oscillator. If the enable is zero, oscillator transitions are blocked and the counter holds its present state. If the enable is one, an authorized clock level reaches the multiplexer. The reset path then removes the permission so that manual operation does not become unrestricted free running.

### 03:35-04:35 - State allocation and state diagram

**Speaker:** Đoàn Minh Nhật

**On screen:** Show the state diagram and point to each state in sequence.

**Narration:**

> The sequencing core requires ten states. The selected cycle is eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve, zero, one, two, three, and four, then back to eight. States eight through eleven provide four clock intervals of green for entries A and C. State twelve provides one yellow interval. States zero through three provide four green intervals for entries B and D. State four provides one yellow interval. In chronological order, this is a four-one-four-one sequence. Grouped by function, it is the requested four-four-one-one allocation: two four-count green groups and two one-count yellow groups.

> This state assignment is deliberate. The two green windows occupy compact binary ranges, and the adjacent yellow states make the decoder depend only on the two most significant state bits. As a result, the lower two bits count within a green window without changing the lamp color.

### 04:35-05:35 - Present-state table and JK excitation method

**Speaker:** Đoàn Minh Nhật

**On screen:** Show the present-state/next-state table, then the corrected JK characteristic and excitation table.

**Narration:**

> We first wrote the present state and required next state for every valid count. We then used the JK excitation relationships. A zero-to-zero transition requires J equal to zero and K as a don't-care. A zero-to-one transition requires J equal to one. A one-to-zero transition requires K equal to one. A one-to-one transition requires K equal to zero. Applying these conditions to all four state bits produces the complete JK excitation table. This is the formal bridge from the state diagram to the combinational input logic of the flip-flops.

### 05:35-06:35 - Karnaugh maps and minimized equations

**Speaker:** Đoàn Minh Nhật

**On screen:** Show the four K-map figures, then Table 11 and the counter crop.

**Narration:**

> Each J and K input is minimized with a four-variable Karnaugh map. The rows use Q three and Q two, while the columns use Q one and Q zero in Gray order. Required transitions are combined with permissible don't-care cells. The minimized results are: J three and K three both equal Q two; J two equals Q one times Q zero; K two equals one; J one and K one both equal Q zero; J zero equals the complement of Q two; and K zero equals one.

> Only one explicit two-input AND gate is needed for the counter excitation. Q two and Q zero are shared directly, the complemented Q two output is already available from the flip-flop, and two K inputs are tied high. This is the main hardware advantage of the selected state order.

### 06:35-07:25 - Lamp decoder and safety property

**Speaker:** Đoàn Minh Nhật

**On screen:** Show the decoder truth table, decoder crop, and four signal heads.

**Narration:**

> The lamp decoder uses only Q three and Q two. When Q three is one and Q two is zero, entries A and C are green. When both bits are one, A and C are yellow. When Q three is zero and Q two is zero, entries B and D are green. When Q three is zero and Q two is one, B and D are yellow. The red outputs are complementary pair-selection signals.

> The two green equations contain Q three and its complement respectively. Their product is therefore always zero. This proves that the A-C green output and the B-D green output cannot be asserted at the same time, including in unused counter states. Each decoded signal is duplicated to the two opposite entries, so A matches C and B matches D exactly.
